Gemini Carbon vs Hydrogen vs Neon vs Argon: One Family, Different Jobs

Google shipped the Gemini family in quick succession, and the naming is the tell. Argon arrived first, Carbon followed, then Hydrogen, and Neon is the newest. They are not successive versions of the same model getting steadily better. They are different answers to the same question: how much thinking should a task buy, and how fast should it finish?

The short version: Hydrogen is the fast everyday worker, Argon is the general-purpose frontier model that started the family, Carbon is the deep-reasoning flagship for the hardest long-horizon work, and Neon is the new middle that tries to give you Carbon-level results at Hydrogen-like speed.

This post explains the concepts behind the family, not the scoreboard. No benchmark figures, no prices, no rankings. Those change with every evaluation run and every pricing update. The ideas underneath them change more slowly, and they are what decide which model fits a job.

The core concept: thinking levels

Every model in the family can think at different depths, and you choose the depth per task. A thinking level is how much internal reasoning the model does before it answers: how many hypotheses it tests, how many times it checks its own work, how far it follows a chain of consequences.

A low thinking level means fast, cheap, and good enough for straightforward work. A high thinking level means slower and more expensive, but more careful on work where a mistake is costly. The default level is the one Google expects most people to use most of the time, and it differs by model because the models are tuned for different jobs.

Hydrogen and Neon offer the widest range of thinking levels, from low to extra high, with high as the default. That range is the point: they are built to cover everything from a quick everyday task to a hard one, without switching models. Carbon and Argon offer a narrower range, with medium as the default. They are built to think deeply by default, and the choice is how much deeper to go.

The practical rule is simple: start at the default, and raise the thinking level only when the work gets harder or the answer matters more. Lower it when the work is routine and speed matters. Thinking is the main cost driver in agentic work, not the words you see in the answer, because the model is billed for the reasoning it does as well as the text it produces.

The lineage: how the family fits together

Argon — the base

Argon is the base model. It was built as a frontier model for software engineering and knowledge work: long, multi-step tasks across code, documents, finance, legal research, and science. Everything else in the family is derived from it.

Carbon — built on Argon

Carbon is built on Argon. It takes the same foundation and spends more thinking on hard logic, with deeper reinforcement-learning loops that let it persist through dead ends, verify subtle bugs across large codebases, and work on open-ended research. It is the model for work that breaks other models, not because it knows more facts, but because it reasons longer about the facts it has.

Hydrogen — adapted from Carbon

Hydrogen is adapted from Carbon. It keeps Carbon's coding discipline and alignment, but it is tuned for rapid execution. It is the execution engine: the model that writes the code, runs the tests, verifies the changes, and moves on to the next step. In multi-model workflows, Carbon can act as the coordinator that plans the work, while Hydrogen powers the worker agents that carry it out in parallel.

Neon — between Hydrogen and Carbon

Neon is adapted from Carbon as well, and it sits between Hydrogen and Carbon. It is built for complex, high-frequency work: production coding, analytical reasoning, and enterprise workflows that run often and cannot wait for the deepest reasoning on every task. Its argument is that near-Carbon quality can arrive fast enough, and cheaply enough per completed task, that you stop noticing the difference.

That lineage matters when you choose. A model adapted from another inherits its strengths and its blind spots. Hydrogen inherits Carbon's discipline. Neon inherits Carbon's reasoning. Carbon inherits Argon's breadth. None of them is a clean break from the rest.

Gemini Hydrogen: the fast one

Hydrogen is the model for work that repeats. Everyday coding, batch fixes, document checks, agent loops that run all day. It is tuned for rapid execution across agentic workflows: programs that split a task into steps, run them in sequence, and check the result at each step.

Its strength is not that it out-thinks the others. It is that it finishes ordinary work before you notice the wait, and it does so with the discipline of a frontier model rather than the shortcuts of a purely fast one. It handles issue resolution, debugging, rapid iteration, and codebase questions with focused, surgical edits, and it adapts cleanly when a tool call fails or a search returns empty.

It is also the most widely available model in the family. Google describes it as bringing Gemini to everyone, rolling out across the Gemini app, Search, AI Studio, the Gemini API, and Gemini for business. That availability is part of its role: it is the default to evaluate first, because it is the easiest to try and the cheapest to run at scale.

The tradeoff is depth. Hydrogen is not built for the hardest long-horizon problems. When a task needs sustained reasoning across many steps, or when a single mistake would be expensive, it is the wrong tool, and the family has other models for that.

Gemini Argon: the one that started it

Argon is the general frontier model. It was built for complex workflows across software engineering, enterprise knowledge work, and cybersecurity defense. Google announced it first to trusted cyber defenders through its Fairwind Program, then made it generally available to developers, enterprises, scientists, and consumers.

Its job has been carved up by its successors. Carbon took the hardest reasoning work. Hydrogen took the fast everyday work. Neon took the complex high-frequency middle. That does not make Argon a bad model. It makes it the model whose role is now the broad middle: good at many things, the specialist at none of them.

If you are already using Argon, nothing in the announcements says it is going away. But in the Gemini app, Neon now takes the Deeper analysis slot previously held by Argon, as reported by 9to5Google, while Carbon holds the code and complex work slot. Before renewing anything built on Argon, check what Neon does to your workload.

Gemini Carbon: the deep thinker

Carbon is Google's strongest model, built for the work that breaks other models: multi-day engineering, open-ended scientific research, and cybersecurity defense. It is designed to take on harder, longer-horizon problems and to persist through dead ends that would stop a faster model.

Its headline strength is cybersecurity. It can find real vulnerabilities buried in real-world software, check which of them are actually exploitable, and patch them at the root cause. That lifecycle — finding, validating, patching — is the core of defensive security work, and Carbon is built for all three stages. It is also the model that generates secure code more reliably than its siblings, because it spends more thinking on the security consequences of the code it writes.

Carbon's safety posture is part of the same design. It is trained to resist indirect prompt injection, where malicious instructions are planted in web pages, emails, documents, or code it accesses. It is trained to reduce shortcuts and loophole exploitation, to cut unsupported claims, and to surface discrepancies to the user instead of guessing. Google reports that in live coding use, its misalignment alerts dropped sharply compared with Argon, which is what you want from a model that runs for days without supervision.

The tradeoff is access and cost. Carbon launched to vetted cyber defenders first, through Google's Fairwind Program, and only later opened to paid tiers and Ultra subscribers. It is the most expensive model in the family per token, and it spends more thinking tokens per turn than Argon on hard logic, so the same task can cost more even at the same per-token price. That is the price of depth, and it is only worth paying when the work actually needs it.

Gemini Neon: the new middle

Neon is the model this comparison could not have included until its launch. Google positions it between Hydrogen and Carbon: frontier-level reasoning for complex, high-frequency workflows — software engineering, knowledge work, and science — faster and more cost-efficiently than Carbon.

Its argument is cost per task, not cost per token. Per token, Neon sits between Hydrogen and Carbon. Per completed task, Google claims it costs less than Carbon, because it spends fewer tokens to get to a comparable answer. That distinction matters for agent work, where the bill is driven by how many reasoning steps a task takes, not by the price of a single token. A cheaper model that needs many retries can cost more than an expensive model that gets it right the first time, and vice versa. Neon is Google's bet that the middle ground wins on that measure.

Neon is also strong on coding agents and science. Google reports strong results on coding-agent evaluations, on science and math reasoning, on terminal-based coding benchmarks, and on agentic evaluations that measure multi-step work. Its cyber variant is where it most directly challenges Carbon: Google reports that it finds vulnerabilities at a level comparable to Carbon, while costing less per challenge and using fewer tokens to validate them.

Its safety posture builds on Carbon's. Google reports that it maintains Carbon-level protections against severe misuse and prompt injection, while declining far less legitimate work. That is the advance that matters most in practice: a model that refuses less of the work you actually want done, without opening the door to the work you do not. Google also reports low rates of cheating, shortcut-taking, and misaligned actions in its internal evaluations, which is what you want from a model that runs long, unsupervised workflows in production.

Neon shipped to all of its surfaces on day one — API, AI Studio, Gemini for business, Antigravity, and the Gemini app for Pro and Ultra subscribers — with no Fairwind-only window for the base model. The cyber variant is the exception: it rolls out to trusted Fairwind defenders first.

Head-to-head: what actually differs

Price. Per token, Hydrogen is the cheapest, Neon sits in the middle, and Carbon and Argon share the same rate, with an introductory period that ends and a higher standard rate after it. But per-token price is the wrong comparison for agent work. What matters is cost per completed task, which depends on how many thinking tokens a model spends, how many retries it needs, and how often it gets the answer right the first time. On that measure, Neon claims an advantage over Carbon, and Hydrogen's speed means it burns fewer tokens on work it can finish in one pass.

Capability. Do not read benchmark scores as a clean ranking. Each model was scored at a different thinking level, on different versions of the same benchmarks, and the benchmarks measure different things. Hydrogen's story is agentic coding speed. Carbon's is vulnerability discovery and patching. Neon's is coding agents and science. The one shared surface, terminal-based coding benchmarks, is reported in different versions across the announcements, so no clean four-way comparison exists yet. Independent evaluators were only just beginning to add Neon to their public leaderboards at launch, so check the latest leaderboard before relying on any ranking.

Speed. Hydrogen is the model Google describes with a clear speed story for everyday work. Neon is described as fast, with fast end-to-end task completion. Carbon is the slowest by design: it spends more thinking tokens per turn than Argon on hard logic. If latency is the constraint, Hydrogen is the safest pick. Neon is the bet that near-Carbon quality can arrive fast enough that you stop noticing.

Safety. All models in the family share the same safety stack, and the two newest — Carbon and Neon — both record strong results on prompt-injection resistance and on cheating evaluations. Neon's advance is not a higher ceiling but a wider door: it declines far less legitimate work than Carbon while keeping the same protections, and Google reports low rates of misalignment and shortcut-taking in its internal evaluations.

Access. This is the starkest contrast in the family. Hydrogen is available to everyone. Argon and Carbon are paid-tier models. Neon is paid-tier too, but unlike Carbon at launch, it shipped to all of its surfaces on day one, with no Fairwind-only window for the base model. The cyber variant is the exception.

Real-life use cases

Hydrogen

  • Clearing the morning code-review queue, drafting a short review comment per pull request
  • Fixing a lint failure across many files and reporting only what still fails
  • Writing unit tests for a new Java endpoint, happy path and edge cases included
  • Triaging an on-call alert for a slow message queue, starting from a likely cause instead of a blank screen
  • Summarising a long design thread into the decision, the alternatives, and the open questions

Argon

  • Designing a new service that talks to an existing platform, sketching boundaries and failure modes first
  • Planning a legacy migration, mapping old calls to new ones and flagging where behaviour changes
  • Reviewing a large pull request across auth, caching, and the database, and spotting how they interact
  • Extracting key dates, obligations, and exceptions from a long contract and cross-checking them against a spreadsheet

Carbon

  • Tracing an intermittent latency spike under load to a single interaction between cache and message queue
  • Auditing an auth service for vulnerabilities, checking which are actually exploitable, and patching the root cause
  • Keeping context across a multi-week codebase migration, picking up where it left off
  • Researching a new protocol and connecting it to the parts of your system it actually affects

Neon

  • Shipping a feature end to end: code, tests, docs, and migration, with what still needs a human decision flagged
  • Running a bug-triage agent loop all day, reproducing the easy ones and filing the hard ones with a clear reproduction
  • Building a weekly report from spreadsheets, PDFs, and charts, with figures traced back to source
  • Doing a pre-launch security review on every service, patching the straightforward vulnerabilities and leaving the hard cases for a human

Who should use which

  • Everyday coding, batch fixes, document checks, and agent loops that run all day: Hydrogen. It is the cheapest to run, the fastest to finish, and the easiest to try. Its strength is repetitive agentic work, and it is good at that work, not just cheap at it.
  • Complex coding, analytical reasoning, and production agent workflows that run often: Neon. It is the new default for work that is too hard for Hydrogen and too frequent for Carbon. The caveat is launch-day freshness: independent tracking was incomplete at launch, and the cost-per-task advantage needs to be tested on your own workloads, not just on Google's benchmark set.
  • The hardest long-horizon projects, multi-day engineering, and open-ended research: Carbon. Nothing else in the family is built to spend this much thinking per turn, and its security work still sets the bar for defensive work. But Google reports Neon performs comparably on several cyber measures at lower cost per task, so Carbon's case rests on whether its deeper thinking shows up on tasks Neon cannot finish.
  • Already on Argon: check what Neon does to your workload before renewing anything. Argon is still available through the API, but in the Gemini app Neon now holds the Deeper analysis slot Argon used to hold.

Open questions

  • Does Neon's launch-day lead survive independent replication? Google's figures are self-reported on benchmarks Google selected, and independent evaluators had only just begun to add Neon to their public leaderboards at launch.
  • Does the cost-per-task advantage hold on your workloads, or only on Google's benchmark set? The claim is a Google estimate at the default thinking level, and cost per task depends on your tasks, not theirs.
  • What happens to Argon? Neon replaces it in the app picker, the API story is unchanged for now, but a model with no unique role in the family rarely stays long.
  • When Carbon's price rises after its introductory period, does anyone still pick it over Neon for non-cyber work? The answer depends on whether Carbon's deeper thinking shows up on tasks Neon cannot finish, and no published benchmark settles that yet.
